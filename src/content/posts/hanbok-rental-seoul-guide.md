Walk toward Gyeongbokgung on almost any day and you'll see dozens of people in bright, flowing <a href="/vocab#hanbok" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">한복</a> (*hanbok*) drifting between the palace gates and the hanok alleys nearby. Almost none of them own it — they rented it an hour earlier from one of dozens of shops within a five-minute walk, and the rental itself comes with a genuinely useful perk: free entry to five of Seoul's royal palaces.

## The free palace entry rule, and what actually qualifies

Wearing hanbok gets you free admission to <a href="/vocab#odae-gunggwol" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">5대 궁궐</a> (*odae gunggwol*), Seoul's five grand palaces: Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Deoksugung, and Gyeonghuigung. Staff at the entrance gate check outfits, and the standard they're checking for is a proper top and bottom — a <a href="/vocab#jeogori" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">저고리</a> (*jeogori*) jacket with a matching <a href="/vocab#chima" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">치마</a> (*chima*) skirt or hanbok pants, not just a robe thrown over street clothes or a jeogori paired with jeans. Any rental shop will hand you a set that clears the bar without you having to think about it; the rule mostly matters if you're mixing pieces yourself.

**One 2026 timing note:** if your trip falls during Chuseok (September 23–27, 2026), several palaces including Gyeongbokgung already offer free admission to everyone for the holiday — so the hanbok rule doesn't add much that week specifically. It matters the rest of the year, when regular admission runs around ₩3,000.

## Where to rent one

Rental shops cluster in a handful of walkable areas, and proximity to a palace is the whole point — you want to be in costume for as little walking as possible before the photos start:

- **Near Gyeongbokgung** (Samcheong-dong and the streets around the palace's west and east sides) — the highest concentration of shops, all within a few minutes of the entrance
- **Bukchon Hanok Village** — shops here lean into the hanok backdrop for photos, slightly fewer crowds than directly at the palace gate
- **Ikseon-dong** — smaller boutique rental shops mixed in with the neighborhood's cafés, a good pick if you're spending the day there anyway
- **Insadong** — a short walk from Gyeongbokgung, with rental shops alongside the area's craft and souvenir shops

Most operate as walk-ins — pick an outfit, get fitted, and you're out the door in 15–20 minutes. Some shops take reservations through Naver or Klook if you want a specific outfit or time slot guaranteed, which is worth doing on weekends or during any national holiday when shops near Gyeongbokgung get busy fast.

## What it costs and what's included

Basic rentals for a roughly 2–4 hour block typically run **₩15,000–25,000** (about $11–18 USD). What's bundled in varies by shop, but commonly includes:

- The hanbok itself, plus any accessories (hair pieces, a small bag)
- Basic hair styling — often a simple braid or updo to match the outfit
- Storage for your own clothes and bags while you're out

Photography add-ons — a staff photographer, professional editing, a printed set — cost extra and are optional. You don't need them; a phone camera and a friend cover most of what people actually want from the experience.

## Traditional vs. "modern hanbok"

Shops usually offer two broad categories, and it's worth knowing the difference before you pick:

- **Traditional hanbok** — historically accurate cuts and proportions, often in the colors and patterns associated with a specific era or royal status. This is what you'll see in sageuk dramas.
- **"Modern hanbok"** (생활한복, *saenghwal hanbok*) — a contemporary reinterpretation with shorter skirts, simpler layers, and easier movement, sometimes mixed with modern fabrics or silhouettes.

Both qualify for free palace entry as long as they keep the top-and-bottom structure. Modern hanbok is generally easier to walk and sit in, which matters more than it sounds like it would after an hour of exploring a palace courtyard.

## Practical tips before you go

- **Wear or bring flat, comfortable shoes.** Palace grounds are gravel and stone, and most hanbok skirts hide your feet anyway — sneakers under a chima are a completely normal choice.
- **Check the weather.** Hanbok fabric runs warm in summer and doesn't block wind in winter; shops often have seasonal weight options, so ask if you're renting outside spring or fall.
- **Don't worry about tying it yourself.** The <a href="/vocab#goreum" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">고름</a> (*goreum*) ribbon tie has a specific knot — staff tie it for you at fitting, and most shops will happily redo it if it comes loose.
- **Go early or late for photos.** Midday sun and midday crowds hit at the same time; the two hours after opening or the last hour before closing are quieter and better lit.
- **Bring cash or a card for the deposit.** Some shops hold a small deposit or ID until the outfit is returned — ask when you check in so there's no surprise at pickup.

## Quick planning checklist

- **Pick a rental shop near the palace you're actually visiting** — Gyeongbokgung's cluster is the default, but Bukchon or Ikseon-dong work if you're spending the day in that neighborhood instead
- **Budget ₩15,000–25,000 and about 2–4 hours** for a standard rental
- **Confirm your outfit has a top and bottom** if you're piecing it together yourself, rather than taking a shop's standard set
- **Skip it during Chuseok if free entry is your only reason** — admission's already free that week regardless
- **Reserve ahead on weekends or holidays** if you want a specific shop, style, or time slot
