## eSIM: skip the airport kiosk line

An eSIM is a digital <a href="/vocab#yusim" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">유심</a>
(*yusim*), SIM card — no physical chip to pick up, swap, or lose. You
buy it, install it on your phone before you leave home, and switch it
on the moment you land. That's it.

**Why I'd recommend this over renting a pocket wifi:**

- No kiosk line at the airport, no deposit to pay, no device to pick
  up or return
- Nothing extra to carry, charge, or worry about losing
- You can set it up entirely before you leave home, so you land
  already connected

Pocket wifi does have its place — if you're travelling as a group and
want to share one connection across several devices, or if your phone
isn't eSIM-compatible, it can still make sense. But for solo or couple
travel, eSIM is far less hassle.

**How I set mine up (using Byte SIM):**

1. Download the app
2. Search for Korea
3. Choose your trip duration
4. Choose how much data (GB) you want
5. Check out
6. Install the eSIM on your phone before you fly
7. Once you land, turn it on — it connects to the local network
   automatically

<img src="/images/posts/seoul-esim-bytesim.jpg" alt="Byte SIM app showing Korea eSIM data and duration options" class="not-prose my-8 w-full max-w-lg mx-auto rounded-xl object-cover" />

**My honest take on Byte SIM:** it's genuinely what I use. It's cheap
— a 2GB/day plan for 5 days runs about $7.90 USD — and I've had zero
connection issues anywhere I've used it in Korea. Once it's installed,
it just shows up
under "My eSIM" in the app so you can check your plan or reinstall it
without digging through phone settings.

One thing to check first: make sure your phone actually supports eSIM
before you buy — most phones from the last few years do, but it's
worth a quick check in your settings.

## Navigation apps: why Google Maps isn't enough here

Here's something worth knowing before you land: Google Maps still
doesn't work well for actual navigation in Korea. South Korea has long
restricted the export of high-precision map data over security
concerns, and while the government conditionally approved Google's
request to export that data back in February 2026, the functionality
still hasn't caught up — as of now, Google Maps still can't reliably
give turn-by-turn walking or driving directions in Korea, and business
listings are often outdated.

That doesn't mean Google Maps is useless — it's still fine for
searching places and reading reviews. Just don't rely on it to
actually get you somewhere.

### The app to actually navigate with: Naver Map

This is the one my Korean husband swears by, and it's the local
standard for a reason:

- Accurate walking and transit <a href="/vocab#gilchatgi" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">길찾기</a>
  (*gilchatgi*), directions, including subway exits and real-time bus
  tracking
- Full English interface, so there's no translation stress
- Easy to set up and navigate even if it's your first time using it

The interface itself is what sold me — it's genuinely intuitive even
on a first use. Search a place like "Gwanghwamun" and you get tabs to
filter between Places, Buses, and Stops, with every result labeled in
both English and Korean so you're never guessing which pin is which.
Subway exits are numbered right on the map, restaurants and landmarks
are colour-coded by category, and a "Search here" button lets you
re-center results as you pan around — small touches, but they add up
to an app that doesn't fight you.

### Bonus app: Kakao T (or really, K.RIDE)

My husband is Korean, and Kakao T is what he's always used to order a
<a href="/vocab#taeksi" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">택시</a>
(*taeksi*), taxi, so it's what he told me to download too. Once I
actually opened it, though, it didn't fully make sense for me — it's
built around logging in with a Kakao account, which is the normal
path for a Korean resident but not exactly a smooth one for a
tourist.

Look closer at that login screen and there's a small, easy-to-miss
link at the bottom: "Use Taxi Service for Foreigners." Tap that
instead, and it hands you off to a separate app called K.RIDE
(powered by Kakao Mobility), built specifically to help tourists book
taxis without needing to set up a full Kakao account first. That's
the one to actually use.
