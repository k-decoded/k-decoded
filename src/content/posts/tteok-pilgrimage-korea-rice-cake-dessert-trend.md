A rice cake is a small thing to plan your whole morning around. In Korea
right now, people are doing exactly that.

Fruit-filled rice cakes, yogurt cream and chocolate have turned tteok into
the reason for the trip. SBS's November 2 report showed customers queuing
late into the night and refreshing reservation pages for a chance to buy
the desserts. Some waits stretched to ten hours.

The name for this is
<a href="/vocab#tteokjisullye" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">떡지순례</a>
(*tteokjisullye*): a rice-cake pilgrimage. Think café hopping, except the
place you're crossing town for sells something you can finish in a few bites.

## What's different about these rice cakes?

If tteok makes you think of spicy tteokbokki, this is another part of the
family. The current dessert wave is full of soft wrappers, generous
fillings and the sort of cross-section that makes you stop scrolling.

One of the big names is **Hanjeongseon (한정선)**, the Seongsu-born brand
known for filled
<a href="/vocab#chapssaltteok" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">찹쌀떡</a>
(*chapssaltteok*), glutinous-rice cakes. Fruit and yogurt are a big part of
the appeal. Yogurt chains and cafés have picked up the combination too.

Then there's **pizza seolgi (피자설기)**: steamed rice cake with pizza-style
toppings. It's a different proposition from a stretchy filled rice cake.
You're choosing the rice-cake base as much as the topping, so don't expect
everything under the tteok label to have the same bite.

The Korean word that keeps coming up is
<a href="/vocab#jjondeukhada" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">쫀득하다</a>
(*jjondeukhada*), pleasantly chewy and slightly sticky. That texture,
paired with familiar dessert flavours, is what ties much of this wave together.

## The numbers behind the queues

This has spread beyond a few photogenic shops. Embrain's receipt-panel
estimates put packaged-tteok spending across major retail channels at
**₩151.9 billion** in the year through August 2026, up **31.2%** year on
year. Spending by people in their 20s and younger rose **45.5%**.

Those figures measure the retail channels in the panel, rather than every
rice cake sold in Korea. Still, they explain why convenience stores are
making room for more of them. A dessert people will travel for is also a
dessert a chain would like to sell around the corner.

## Trying it without spending the day in line

**For the fruit-filled version:** look up Hanjeongseon and choose a branch
before making plans. October reporting described queues at its Seongsu
and department-store locations. Check that branch's latest menu and waiting
instructions; a video of someone buying a particular filling isn't a
promise it'll be available when you arrive.

**For a convenience-store introduction:** GS25's Hanjeongseon yogurt
chapssaltteok was listed at **₩3,900** in late October. It uses a cream
filling and doesn't contain the whole fruit found in some specialist-shop
versions. CU has also introduced fresh chocolate mochi and matcha mochi
pavé chocolate. These are different takes on the chewy-dessert idea, so
choose the flavour you actually want to eat.

**For something savoury:** GS25 announced a nationwide launch of
**Seolbing Pizza Seolgi on November 5**, with pizza sauce, cheese and
pepperoni on steamed white rice cake. That's still upcoming as of this
post, and individual branches may have different stock.

**A November 3 update:** skip the GS25 Hanjeongseon **pistachio** version
you may have seen in older recommendations. GS25 stopped sales on October
30 after a coliform-standard failure; the reported recall covers 80g
packs manufactured October 23, 2026. The chain also announced disposal of
remaining store stock and refunds for customers. See the
[October 30 report](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20261030_0003848525)
for the product details.

## Two words to check before you queue

<a href="/vocab#opeunreon" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">오픈런</a>
(*opeunreon*) means arriving before opening to get a popular item. Add
tteok and you get 떡 오픈런, the rice-cake version. But some shops use
online reservations instead, so setting an early alarm won't necessarily
get you a place.

The other word is
<a href="/vocab#pumjeol" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">품절</a>
(*pumjeol*): sold out. Worth spotting before you commit to the line.

At the counter, try:

**요거트 찹쌀떡 아직 있어요?**<br />
*Yogeoteu chapssaltteok ajik isseoyo?*<br />
“Do you still have yogurt rice cakes?”

And when you've found your choice:

**이거 하나 주세요.**<br />
*Igeo hana juseyo.*<br />
“One of these, please.”

Our [Korean restaurant phrases](/blog/korean-restaurant-phrases) cover
the rest. If you're making a snack afternoon of it, the
[kkosup latte guide](/blog/kkosup-latte-korea-coffee-trend) gives you
something to drink alongside it.

Choose one shop you're curious about and keep an alternative nearby.
Trying a rice cake is an easy addition to a Seoul afternoon. Whether it
deserves a ten-hour wait is a decision best made after you've tasted one.
