Korea has had ballet core, gorpcore and, just last week,
[Han Yoo-joo core](/blog/han-yoo-joo-core-2000s-kdrama-fashion). The
newest one is named after a department store.
<a href="/vocab#hyeonbaek" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">현백</a>
(*Hyeonbaek*) is what everyone calls Hyundai Department Store, so 현백코어,
Hyunbaek core, means "Hyundai Department Store core."

The way people online describe it is very specific: the mood of a
<a href="/vocab#bujatjip-ttal" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">부잣집 딸</a>
(*bujatjip ttal*), a rich family's daughter, who came to the department
store and found there's nothing left to buy. She isn't excited. She's a
little bored. She already owns everything.

## Where it came from

Hyundai didn't come up with the name. Young shoppers did, and the hashtag
took off on Instagram and Pinterest over the past month. Korean outlets
from Newsis to the Hankook Ilbo have picked it up, and The Korea Times
covered it on October 16.

It makes sense that it's Hyundai and not Lotte or Shinsegae. Each of
Korea's big three department stores has its own image. Lotte is the
everyday one with branches across the country. Shinsegae is classic
luxury, with VIP tiers for people spending over ₩120 million a year.
Hyundai has spent the last few years chasing young, wealthy shoppers,
and it worked:

- People in their 20s and 30s made up **46.7%** of Hyundai's visitors
  from January to September this year, up from 37% in 2020.
- Shoppers in their 20s grew **38.1%** year on year.
- Its VIP club for 20- to 45-year-olds who spend ₩30 million or more a
  year has more than doubled in members since 2024 (Hankyung).

Most of that comes down to one building:
<a href="/vocab#deo-hyeondae" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">더현대</a>
(*Deo Hyeondae*), The Hyundai Seoul in Yeouido. It's known for its
pop-ups and exhibitions, and it gives a huge share of its floor space to
an indoor garden where nobody is trying to sell you anything. For a lot
of young Koreans, it's become *the* place to spend a weekend.

## How to get the look

If someone tells you their
<a href="/vocab#chugumi" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">추구미</a>
(*chugumi*), the style they're going for, is Hyunbaek core, this is what
they mean:

- **Fabric over logos.** Cashmere knits, silk blouses, crisp shirts in
  neutral colors and well-cut trousers. Retailers say demand for
  pricey, fabric-focused contemporary brands and cashmere and silk lines
  is up. A visible logo is the fastest way to break the look.
- **Small, classic jewelry.** A thin chain or small studs, nothing big
  or flashy.
- **Barely-there makeup.** Like you just popped out to the corner shop.
- **The attitude.** This matters most. You're relaxed, never rushed, and
  money isn't on your mind. It's peak
  <a href="/vocab#kkuankku" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">꾸안꾸</a>
  (*kkuankku*), "dressed up but not dressed up."

The go-to references are the
<a href="/vocab#sabok" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">사복</a>
(*sabok*), off-duty outfits, of actresses **Moon Ga-young** and **Go
Youn-jung**. Girls' Generation's Sooyoung has also posted about the
keyword.

If this sounds like "old money" or "quiet luxury," you're right. Korean
fashion writers describe it as the global old-money look localized to a
Korean department store. The Korea Times notes it's a bit more relaxed
than old money, though it keeps some polish.

## Why it's catching on now

It's not only about clothes. Korean experts link it to how young people
feel about money:

- An economics professor at Sangmyung University told The Korea Times
  that self-made success stories have lost their pull, and admiration
  for inherited wealth has taken their place.
- An Inha University consumer psychology professor called it the
  "panoplie effect," where buying the things linked to a social class
  makes you feel part of it. She ties it to falling social mobility.
- Industry analysts also point to high prices and a slow economy, which
  have cooled Korea's luxury-logo boom. Showing off is out. Looking like
  you don't need to show off is in.

Not everyone's a fan. Commenters have asked why a nice outfit needs to
be labeled with a department store and "rich daughter" at all. Others
say it sells wealth itself as a style and leaves people feeling worse
off. One recurring question: is *looking rich* really the goal now?

## Seeing it for yourself

You don't have to buy cashmere to check out the trend. The Hyundai Seoul
is free to walk around and is one of Seoul's best rainy-day spots.

- **Getting there:** It's a few minutes' walk from Yeouido Station
  (Lines 5 and 9). Our
  [Seoul transportation guide](/blog/getting-around-seoul-transportation-guide)
  covers the subway basics.
- **What to see:** the indoor garden, the rotating pop-ups and the
  basement food hall. Pop-ups change every few weeks, so check what's
  on before you go.
- **Go on a weekday** if you can. Weekends get packed, especially when
  a popular pop-up is on.
- **Shopping anyway?** Spend ₩15,000 or more and you can claim the VAT
  back. Our [tax-free shopping guide](/blog/tax-free-shopping-korea-olive-young-spao)
  explains how.

And if you're in Yeouido on November 28 or 29, KBS Hall is one stop away
on Line 9, where [Pokémon The Orchestra](/blog/pokemon-the-orchestra-30th-anniversary-kbs-hall)
is playing.
