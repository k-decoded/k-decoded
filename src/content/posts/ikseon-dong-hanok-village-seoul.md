We went to Ikseon-dong four days ago, on a <a href="/vocab#pyeongil" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">평일</a>
(*pyeongil*), weekday, and it genuinely surprised us — we'd braced for
the wall-to-wall crowds we'd read about, and instead could actually
walk the alleys at our own pace, stop wherever we wanted, and get into
places without a wait. If your schedule is flexible at all, a weekday
visit is worth planning for.

## A hanok village built for regular people

Most of what people picture when they hear "hanok village" in Seoul is
Bukchon — grand, aristocratic homes from the Joseon dynasty. Ikseon-dong
is a different story. It was developed in the 1920s, during the
Japanese colonial period, by a Korean entrepreneur and independence
activist named Jeong Se-gwon, who was also racing to keep Korean
neighborhoods from being absorbed as Japanese settlers expanded north
of Cheonggyecheon. Rather than building for the aristocracy, he
designed a new, compact style — the "urban <a href="/vocab#hanok" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">한옥</a>
(*hanok*)" — meant for Seoul's emerging middle class: merchants,
teachers, mid-level civil servants. These homes were smaller than
Bukchon's, but modern for their time, with running water, electricity,
and an indoor toilet instead of the traditional outdoor one.

By the early 2000s, the city had drawn up plans to tear the neighborhood
down for high-rises. That plan was scrapped, and in 2015 Seoul put
forward a district plan focused on tourism and revitalization instead.
What's there now is the result: the same century-old hanok shells and
the same tangle of <a href="/vocab#golmok" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">골목</a>
(*golmok*), alleys, so narrow in places that two people can barely pass
side by side, but with cafes, bars, dessert shops, and small boutiques
tucked inside instead of family homes.

## The cafes are the main event — and some aren't cheap

Ikseon-dong's cafes are genuinely beautiful, and there are a lot of
them packed into a small area — every alley seems to have another one
worth ducking into, if only to take a photo of the space itself. Old
hanok roof beams left exposed, tiny courtyards turned into seating,
interiors that lean maximalist or minimalist depending on which door
you pick. It's the kind of neighborhood where "we just went in for a
picture" is a completely normal reason to sit down somewhere.

That said, be ready for the prices. Some of the more photogenic cafes
charge well above what you'd pay for the same coffee or dessert
elsewhere in Seoul — you're paying for the space and the atmosphere as
much as the drink. Worth it for at least one stop, but budget for it
rather than assuming Seoul café prices across the board.

## Small boutiques worth slowing down for

Beyond the cafes, Ikseon-dong is full of small, niche boutiques — the
kind of one-room shops selling things you won't find in a department
store: independent jewelry designers, ceramics, stationery, concept
stores that don't fit neatly into a single category. It's a good
neighborhood to walk slowly rather than beeline toward a landmark.

One stop we'd genuinely recommend: **Royal Water**, a <a href="/vocab#hyangsu" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">향수</a>
(*hyangsu*), perfume, shop just a couple minutes from Jongno 3-ga Station. They stock dozens of
different scents to sample and layer, plus perfume keychains and
cases, and they had a 3+1 deal running when we visited — buy three,
get a fourth free — which made it an easy place to actually commit to
buying rather than just smelling our way through the testers. The shop
assistant who helped us spoke excellent English and Japanese, which
made the whole visit easy even with the language barrier that trips
some people up elsewhere.

## Lunch: pot rice at Damsot

For lunch we stopped at 담솥 (Damsot), specifically the Ikseon-dong
direct-run branch. It specializes in <a href="/vocab#sotbap" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">솥밥</a>
(*sotbap*), pot rice — rice cooked and served in its own small hot pot,
topped with everything from eggplant to steak to salmon. The menu board
out front lists more than a dozen variations, roughly ₩13,000–21,000
each.

<img src="/images/posts/ikseon-dong-damsot-menu-board.jpg" alt="The Damsot Ikseon-dong menu board listing pot rice varieties and prices, next to the restaurant's wooden signage" class="not-prose my-8 w-full max-w-md mx-auto rounded-xl object-cover" />

We got the fried chicken pot rice and the mackerel pot rice. Both were
genuinely delicious, and the portions were far more generous than we
expected for the price — we left properly full rather than needing a
second stop.

## A traditional teahouse, for something calmer

After lunch, we stopped into 뜰안 (Tteuran), a traditional tea and
dessert cafe that's been open since 2009 — the kind of place serving
<a href="/vocab#bingsu" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">빙수</a>
(*bingsu*), shaved ice, and <a href="/vocab#tteok" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">떡</a>
(*tteok*), rice cake, alongside herbal drinks and teas marketed as
good for your body rather than just something sweet. It was a nice
change of pace from the trendier, more Instagram-built cafes nearby —
slower, quieter, and it felt like an actual break rather than another
photo stop. If you want one moment in Ikseon-dong that isn't about the
aesthetics, look for one of these traditional spots.

## Is it worth the visit?

Yes — especially if you can go on a weekday. Ikseon-dong packs a lot
into a small footprint: real history behind the hanok facades, cafes
worth photographing even if you don't stay, boutiques worth browsing
slowly, a perfume shop worth the detour, and at least one traditional
teahouse worth sitting down in properly. Just go in expecting to spend
more than you would elsewhere for the cafe experience, and you won't
be caught off guard.
