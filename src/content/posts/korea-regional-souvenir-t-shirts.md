The Korea souvenir everyone wants right now isn't a K-beauty haul or a
fridge magnet. It's a cheap-looking T-shirt with a cartoon chestnut on it.
Across Korea, small-town shops are selling deliberately kitschy shirts about
the one thing their area is known for: chestnuts, oysters, potatoes. Travelers
are building whole day trips around buying one.

## The shirt that started it: Gongju's "bamti"

Gongju is a small city in South Chungcheong Province, about an hour and a
half south of Seoul by express bus. It was a Baekje kingdom capital, and it's
famous for its chestnuts.

![The stone walls and gate pavilion of Gongsanseong Fortress in Gongju under a blue summer sky](/images/posts/souvenir-shirts-gongsanseong-fortress.jpg)

<p class="not-prose -mt-4 mb-8 text-xs text-gray-400">Geumseoru Gate at Gongsanseong Fortress, a short walk from Sanseong Market. In May 2026, Lee Tae-hee, who runs a shop called 제일유통 (Jeil Yutong) in
Gongju's Sanseong Market, started selling a T-shirt with a plump chestnut
character on the front, dressed up with a crown and jewels. Within a month it had gone viral. It now sells **over
1,000 shirts a day on weekends**. Lee says tourists wear it around the market,
locals ask where they got it, and then the locals buy one too.

The joke only works in Korean, and it works on two levels.

**1. 밤티.**
<a href="/vocab#bamti" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">밤티</a>
(*bamti*) is Gen Z slang for something ugly, tacky, or just a little off. It
reportedly started with a user called "Bamti" in the avatar game LINE Play,
whose long-haired, bearded avatar people found hilariously ugly. It spread
on X in 2025, and now people use it for anything from an outfit to a haircut. But read
literally, 밤 is *chestnut* and 티 is *tee*. A chestnut tee is, by
definition, a bamti.

**2. 공주.**
<a href="/vocab#gongju" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">공주</a>
(*gongju*) is the city's name, but it's also the everyday word for
*princess*. So "공주 밤티" can mean "Gongju chestnut tee" or "ugly princess."
The crown is the giveaway. Wearing one is a self-own and a sightseeing receipt at the same time, and
that's exactly why people buy it.

## Tongyeong's oyster shirt comes in a seafood tray

Tongyeong, on the south coast, produces most of Korea's
<a href="/vocab#gul" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">굴</a>
(*gul*), oysters. Four young women from the city started a lifestyle brand
called **통영아가씨클럽 (Tongyeong Agassi Club, or TACLUB)** because they felt
the town had nothing made for people their age.

Their first product was an "I ♡ TY" shirt where the heart is an oyster. The
packaging is the gag: shirts come shrink-wrapped on a foam seafood tray, with
a hand-lettered price card and a supermarket-style label, like you just
bought them at the fish market. One oyster-print shirt is labeled
굴랑해 (*gulranghae*), a mash-up of 굴 and 사랑해 (*saranghae*, "I love you").

## More shirts to look for

- **Gangneung — potato.**
  <a href="/vocab#gamja" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">감자</a>
  (*gamja*) is Gangwon Province's pride. Potato-character shops such as
  삶은감자 (*Salmeun Gamja*, "Boiled Potato") near downtown Gangneung sell potato
  tees alongside figures, keyrings, and potato-shaped mugs.
- **Daejeon — Kkumdori.** The space-fairy mascot of the 1993 Daejeon Expo is now
  a retro favorite, and Kkumdori shirts are part of the same wave. Pair it
  with a bread run to Sungsimdang.
- **Gyeongju — Seokguram.** A Supreme-logo parody built around the
  1,300-year-old Buddha of Seokguram Grotto. Streetwear meets a UNESCO site.

## Why these shirts are working

For years, travel in Korea has been about where to eat. Hanyang University
professor Lee Hoon told the Korea Times that people aren't buying these
shirts because they need clothes. They're buying the joke, and he argues that
smaller cities need to give visitors something fun as well as useful.

It's a little like "I ♥ NY": a T-shirt that makes a place feel like
somewhere worth going. The difference is that these were started by market
shopkeepers and young locals, not a tourism board. And for small markets that
were losing foot traffic, a shirt that sells a thousand times a weekend day
counts as an economic plan.
