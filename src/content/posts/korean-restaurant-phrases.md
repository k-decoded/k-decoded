Ordering a meal in Korea doesn't require fluency — it requires about
six words and the confidence to say them a little too loudly across a
restaurant. Table service here runs differently from what a lot of
first-time visitors expect, and knowing that upfront saves you from
sitting there waiting for a server who's never coming over on their
own.

## Calling a server: 저기요

The single most useful word on this list. Servers in Korea don't
hover or check in — you call them over. <a href="/vocab#jeogiyo" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">저기요</a>
(*jeogiyo*), roughly "excuse me!" or "over here!," said at normal
restaurant volume (louder than it feels natural to a lot of visitors
at first), is how you get a server's attention to order, ask a
question, or request the bill. Many restaurants also have a call
button on the table — press it once and wait, no need to shout.

## Ordering: 주문

<img src="/images/posts/korean-restaurant-ordering-menu.png" alt="A customer pointing at a menu while a server takes her order by hand, with a chalkboard menu of Korean dishes and prices on the wall" class="not-prose my-8 w-full rounded-xl object-cover" />

<a href="/vocab#jumun" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">주문</a>
(*jumun*) means "order." "주문할게요" (*jumunhalgeyo*) — "I'd like to
order" — is what you say once a server's at your table. If you're
pointing at a menu item or photo rather than reading the name aloud,
"이거 주세요" (*igeo juseyo*), "I'll have this," covers you completely.
Between those two phrases, you can order almost anything without
knowing a single dish name — useful the first time you're ordering
<a href="/blog/busan-vs-seoul-which-trip-fits-you" class="text-brand-700 underline">dwaeji gukbap in Busan</a>
or anything else you can't yet pronounce.

## Dine-in or to go: 포장

The same word that means "packaging" or "gift-wrapping" when you're
<a href="/blog/korean-shopping-phrases" class="text-brand-700 underline">shopping</a>
does double duty at restaurants: <a href="/vocab#pojang" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">포장</a>
(*pojang*) means "to go." Say "포장이요" (*pojangiyo*) and you're
ordering takeout; say "여기서 먹을게요" (*yeogiseo meogeulgeyo*), "I'll eat
here," if a server assumes otherwise. Worth asking upfront — not every
restaurant offers both options.

## Free extras: 반찬 and 리필

<img src="/images/posts/korean-restaurant-banchan.png" alt="A spread of small metal bowls with Korean banchan side dishes, including kimchi, radish kimchi, seasoned greens, and japchae noodles, on a BBQ table" class="not-prose my-8 w-full rounded-xl object-cover" />

Korean meals come with <a href="/vocab#banchan" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">반찬</a>
(*banchan*), small side dishes — kimchi, seasoned vegetables, whatever
the kitchen's serving that day — set down before your main dish
arrives, at no extra cost. When they run low, <a href="/vocab#ripil" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">리필</a>
(*ripil*), refill, is the word to know: "반찬 리필 되나요?" (*banchan ripil
doenayo?*), "can I get a refill on the side dishes?" Water is usually
self-serve from a cooler or dispenser near the entrance — free, and
not something you need to ask a server for.

## Asking for the bill: 계산

<img src="/images/posts/korean-restaurant-paying-bill.png" alt="A customer tapping a card to pay at a Korean restaurant counter, with a card terminal and a 'review event' sign offering a free drink for a receipt review" class="not-prose my-8 w-full rounded-xl object-cover" />

<a href="/vocab#gyesan" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">계산</a>
(*gyesan*) means payment or checkout. "계산해 주세요" (*gyesanhae
juseyo*) — "check, please" — is what you say, but two things trip
people up: tipping isn't expected anywhere, and a lot of restaurants
want you to pay at a counter near the exit rather than at the table.
If you're not sure which applies, "계산이요" (*gyesan-iyo*) called out as
you're ready to leave prompts a server to tell you where to pay.

Keep an eye out for a "리뷰 이벤트" (*ribyu ibenteu*), review event, sign
near the register too — plenty of restaurants offer a free drink or
extra side for leaving a receipt review, usually on Naver Map.

## Quick reference

Everything from this post, in one place:

- **저기요** (*jeogiyo*) — "Excuse me!" (to call a server)
- **주문할게요** (*jumunhalgeyo*) — "I'd like to order"
- **이거 주세요** (*igeo juseyo*) — "I'll have this"
- **포장이요** (*pojangiyo*) — "To go, please"
- **여기서 먹을게요** (*yeogiseo meogeulgeyo*) — "I'll eat here"
- **반찬 리필 되나요?** (*banchan ripil doenayo?*) — "Can I get a refill on the side dishes?"
- **계산해 주세요** (*gyesanhae juseyo*) — "Check, please"

## Worth remembering

Six words and a handful of short phrases cover a full meal out: getting
someone's attention, ordering without knowing a dish name, choosing
dine-in or takeout, asking for more banchan, and paying. Pair this
with our <a href="/blog/korean-shopping-phrases" class="text-brand-700 underline">shopping phrases guide</a>
and you've got most of a day in Korea covered without needing to speak
the language beyond a survival level.
