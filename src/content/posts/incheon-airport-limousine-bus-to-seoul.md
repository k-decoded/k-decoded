We always take the train in from <a href="/vocab#gonghang" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">공항</a>
(*gonghang*), airport — it's the default, it's fast, and it's what
everyone recommends. This trip, for the first time, we took the
<a href="/vocab#rimujin-beoseu" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">리무진 버스</a>
(*rimujin beoseu*), limousine bus, instead, and it's genuinely
underrated. Nobody talks about it the way they talk about AREX, but
after one ride we're not sure we're going back.

## Why it beats the train, honestly

The train is quick, but it comes with a specific kind of stress:
hauling your own <a href="/vocab#jim" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">짐</a>
(*jim*), luggage, through crowded carriages, then up and down stairs
at every transfer, trying not to block anyone while you wrestle a
suitcase onto an escalator. It's manageable, but it's not relaxing.

The limousine bus removes all of that. You hand your suitcase to the
staff once, at the curb, and you don't touch it again until Seoul.
There are no stairs, no transfers, no standing room — just a wide,
comfortable coach seat for the entire ride. After a long flight, that
difference is bigger than it sounds.

## How it actually works

The process is simple enough that it doesn't need much explanation,
but here's exactly how it went for us:

1. **Buy your ticket** at a <a href="/vocab#maepyoso" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">매표소</a>
   (*maepyoso*), kiosk, or staffed counter in the arrivals area. Kiosks
   take credit cards and run in English, Korean, Chinese, and
   Japanese; counters take cash too if you need it.

2. **Check the gate number printed on your ticket.** That number is
   your <a href="/vocab#jeongnyujang" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">정류장</a>
   (*jeongnyujang*), bus stop — in Terminal 1 it's a letter-and-number
   combo (like 12A) marking a specific spot outside the arrivals hall;
   in Terminal 2, every stop is downstairs at the indoor transportation
   center, so you're never standing outside in the weather.
3. **Wait at your stop and hand your suitcase over** when the bus
   pulls in. The staff loading the under-bus compartment are
   meticulous about it — bags go in fast, organized, and without you
   having to lift a finger.
4. **Get on, find your <a href="/vocab#jwaseok" class="font-korean rounded bg-brand-50 px-1 font-semibold text-brand-700 no-underline hover:bg-brand-100">좌석</a>
   (*jwaseok*), seat,** and that's it. Smooth sailing from there.

## What it costs, and how long it takes

Standard limousine routes run about ₩17,000, with the premium
"K-Limousine" buses slightly higher at around ₩18,000 — those come
with extra legroom and fewer stops. Kids between 6 and 12 ride for
roughly ₩11,000. Trip time is typically 60–90 minutes depending on
your destination in Seoul, though Friday afternoon traffic can add
another 30–60 minutes on top, same as it would in a taxi.

It's not the fastest option on paper — AREX will usually beat it to
Seoul Station — but once you factor in zero stair-hauling, guaranteed
seating, and not having to watch your bags every second, the extra
time stopped feeling like a downside on this trip.

## Would we take it again?

Yes, without much hesitation. If you're arriving with real luggage,
traveling with someone less mobile, or just landed after a long-haul
flight and don't have the energy to fight a suitcase through a train
transfer, the limousine bus is worth the small price difference. We'll
still take the train when we're moving light, but for a proper arrival
with bags, this is our new default.
